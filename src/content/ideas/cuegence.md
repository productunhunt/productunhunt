## How It Started

The idea began with a very reasonable engineering question: schools collect marks, homework, assignments, teacher feedback, curriculum plans, and years of academic history, so why does almost none of it become a useful model of how a student is actually learning?

This is the sort of question that becomes dangerous when asked by someone who enjoys building systems more than selling them. The correct response is to go for a walk. I opened a whiteboard.

## The Pitch

Cuegence was going to be a longitudinal learning-intelligence system for schools.

Not another LMS. Not another ERP. Not a chatbot with a graduation cap pasted onto it.

At the centre was a structured learner model: for every student and every concept, Cuegence would continuously maintain what the evidence suggested they understood, how confident that inference was, what prerequisites might be missing, which misconceptions appeared repeatedly, whether knowledge seemed to be improving or decaying, and which previous interventions had actually helped.

The curriculum itself would become a graph: grade, subject, chapter, concept, sub-concept, prerequisite relationships, learning outcomes, and expected progression. If you have never seen an entire national syllabus rendered as a directed acyclic graph, I can report that it is beautiful, and that nobody else wants to look at it.

Then the evidence would start flowing in.

Question-wise marks from normal exams would map back to concepts. Assignments and homework would become signals. Teacher feedback would become high-weight evidence. Confirmed teaching coverage would tell the model what had actually been taught rather than what the annual plan claimed had been taught — two documents that historically meet only at parent-teacher conferences, and even then reluctantly. Future evaluation systems could contribute more signals without requiring students to live inside another app.

The important part was that an inference never replaced its evidence. Cuegence would preserve provenance, uncertainty, teacher corrections, later observations, and model history so the whole thing remained auditable instead of becoming an educational horoscope generated by an LLM.

Once the longitudinal model existed, useful products fell out of it.

The Teaching Copilot would generate lesson plans using not just the syllabus, but the accumulated state of the actual class. It could suggest prerequisite refreshers, examples, formative questions, pacing changes, homework, and intervention ideas based on what the students appeared to need.

Parents could receive personalized daily or weekly practice through channels they already used instead of installing another student app.

School leadership could see curriculum progress, learning patterns, unresolved uncertainty, intervention outcomes, and academic signals without turning teachers into leaderboard entries.

Direct student PII would stay outside the intelligence layer wherever possible. Learners would be represented by school-scoped pseudonymous identifiers. The system would be model-agnostic. Cross-school benchmarking was forbidden. Teacher rankings were forbidden. Student rankings were forbidden. Everything would be explainable.

By the end, this was no longer a product idea.

It was an architecture diagram with moral principles.

## The Delusion

Technically, Cuegence was magnificent.

It had graphs.

It had longitudinal state.

It had evidence provenance.

It had confidence calibration problems nobody had solved yet.

It had privacy boundaries.

It had a closed feedback loop.

It had enough product definitions to suggest that the product itself might eventually become unnecessary because the documentation had achieved consciousness.

It had everything, in fact, except a single confirmed human being who wanted it.

And the problem sounded important.

Great teachers do form mental models of their students over time. They remember that one child struggles with fractions because multiplication never became automatic. They know which class needs another example before moving on. They notice when a student who normally understands everything suddenly goes quiet.

Software should be able to help preserve and scale some of that context.

The market story also looked plausible. Schools already pay for software. AI in education is everywhere. Generic copilots do not know the learning history of a particular class. Cuegence would.

Then came the inevitable founder sentence:

> "If we can build the longitudinal learner model properly, this could be a real moat."

This sentence has probably caused more unnecessary software to be built than PHP.

There were even several pivots available whenever reality became inconvenient.

If schools did not want analytics, position it as a Teaching Copilot.

If the Teaching Copilot sounded commoditized, emphasize that it was backed by longitudinal intelligence.

If K-12 sales were painful, sell the intelligence layer to coaching companies.

If coaching companies could build it themselves, integrate into LMS platforms.

Moodle plugin. Tutor LMS plugin. APIs. Existing school apps. Parent communication layers.

Every objection had an architectural response.

Unfortunately, customers are not architecture reviewers.

## The Reality Check

I did the deeply unfashionable thing and talked to potential buyers before building it.

Six schools.

Three effectively said they would pay nothing because they did not need it.

Two said something around ₹50,000 per year, in the tone of someone donating to a cause.

One said roughly ₹100 per student per month — if Cuegence could replace enough of the existing school software to justify the cost.

Which was a particularly elegant way of saying, "Your interesting product becomes commercially useful after you turn it into the boring ERP you specifically refused to build."

There was another problem hiding inside every integration strategy.

Cuegence needed signals.

Useful signals.

Continuous signals.

Mapped signals.

Question-wise marks, assignment results, teacher feedback, teaching coverage, intervention outcomes.

That meant integration with whatever the institution already used, and no matter how many times the phrase "plugin ecosystem" appeared in the plan, this was not going to be a one-click install. It was going to be a nine-month install with a WhatsApp group.

The intelligence layer only becomes intelligent after the institution does enough work to feed it intelligence. Until then it is a very well-architected empty database with moral principles.

I considered higher education too, where there are more digital systems and perhaps more budget. The problem got worse. Courses move faster, teacher-student interaction is less consistent, feedback is noisier, and the system would spend increasing amounts of sophistication trying to infer learning from decreasing amounts of evidence — a machine for drawing ever-more-confident conclusions from ever-less data, which already exists and is called LinkedIn.

This was all survivable. Difficult sales, messy integrations, imperfect signals — companies have been built on worse.

Then I pitched Cuegence to my dad.

He has been a teacher for more than forty years.

I explained the learner model. The concept graph. The evidence loop. The Teaching Copilot. The idea that software could continuously understand what each student knows and help the teacher decide what to do next.

His reaction, translated into the polite version, was approximately:

> "What is this? I have taught for forty years. I know how to teach children. I know what they know. I know how they will learn. Why would I use software for this?"

There are moments in product discovery when a sophisticated objection requires careful analysis.

This was not one of them.

I had spent weeks designing a system to computationally reconstruct the mental model an experienced teacher already carries around in his head — and my target user was asking why I wanted him to maintain a second, more expensive copy.

Forty years of longitudinal learner modelling, running entirely on tea and repetition, with zero integration cost and full offline support. My competition was my own father, and he had a forty-year head start and no cloud bill.

The problem was real.

The engineering was interesting.

The model might even have worked.

But "interesting engineering problem" and "good business opportunity" had quietly been sitting in different classrooms the entire time.

## Why It Stays Unbuilt

Because the strongest version of the product required schools and teachers to feed a complex intelligence system so it could help them do something many of them already believe they know how to do.

Six schools gave me pricing data. My dad gave me the answer.

Customer discovery: one sentence. Time to insight: forty years, but they were his, not mine.

## Unwanted Bonus

**Cuegence Teacher Pro.**

Every evening, the teacher spends ten minutes entering observations about the class so the AI can analyze them overnight and send a beautifully formatted notification the next morning:

**"Based on 47 longitudinal signals, you may want to revise fractions today."**

The teacher looks at the notification.

The teacher had already planned to revise fractions today.

The teacher enters that as feedback so the model can improve.

The model files it under evidence provenance.

Confidence: increasing. Usefulness: longitudinal.
